Unemployment and the Meaning of Leisure Time

Published on: 09 September 2024

Being involuntarily unemployed does not really mean not doing anything at all productive. Applying for jobs, scaling up skills, interview preparation, side projects, stress, mental load, and other juggling consume time. In economics, “leisure” means time not spent in paid work. Measured non-work time is higher for the unemployed, but experienced or freely chosen leisure may be lower than it appears.

How close is the leisure period when you are employed and when you are involuntarily unemployed? What is the trade-off between those who are actively seeking a job and do not want to be unemployed, and those who are employed and seek leisure? It may be almost equal, or rather, the job seeker may have less meaningful leisure than the employed person.

Unemployed people continuously search for jobs, work on upgrading their skills, or plan to start something on their own, even during weekends. The pressure to improve employability often blurs the distinction between work and leisure, making free time feel less restorative and more like an extension of the job search process.

In contrast, employed people, after the usual 9-to-5 job, often seek more leisure and look forward to weekends and holidays. Their leisure time is generally more clearly defined because it follows a period of productive work and income generation. As a result, leisure frequently carries a stronger sense of reward and relaxation.

The marginal utility of leisure for employed people is higher than for unemployed people. For the employed person, leisure is scarce, so the marginal value of an extra hour of leisure may be high. Time away from work becomes more valuable precisely because it is limited.

For the involuntarily unemployed person, total non-work time is larger, but the utility derived from it may be lower because it is constrained by anxiety, stigma, income loss, and job-search pressure. Although more hours are technically available, those hours may not be experienced as genuine leisure if they are overshadowed by uncertainty about the future.

This distinction highlights an important economic and psychological insight: the quantity of leisure and the quality of leisure are not the same. Time that is voluntarily chosen and free from financial stress often provides greater satisfaction than time that is available only because one lacks employment. Therefore, comparing employed and unemployed individuals solely through the amount of non-work time can be misleading.
